Most salon sites on this coast are a business card with a phone number on
it. Nice photos, a "call to book" line, and prices that never appear
anywhere. A salon's whole business is time and money: whose chair, how
long, how much. We think both belong on the page, in the site's own voice,
where somebody can settle the question at eleven at night without calling
anybody.

Magnolia & Mane is the specimen that argues it. It is a fictional salon in
Ocean Springs, built and deployed as a working site so the argument has
something to stand on. There is no client here and no engagement to
narrate. Everything below is a claim about the genre that you can go and
check on the live site.

## Prices that don't hide

Signature Cut & Style, $65. Full Color & Gloss, from $120. Gel Manicure,
$45. Classic Pedicure, $55. Real numbers, on the page, not a "call for
pricing" that quietly filters out anyone unwilling to make a phone call
just to find out whether they can afford it.

A service priced by variables says so honestly. Color is "from $120", and
the page explains why in plain words: it scales with length and density,
and the stylist confirms the number at consultation before any color goes
on. That is the whole trick. A range stated up front reads as candor; the
same range discovered in the chair reads as a bait and switch.

## Booking that doesn't leave

The booking widget on a salon site is usually the moment the site gives
up. Different font, different chrome, and a redirect that dumps the
visitor on somebody else's domain to finish the one thing they came to do.

This specimen keeps that on its own page. Booking lives at a route the
site owns, wrapped in the site's own header, type, and color, and the
scheduling calendar loads in place rather than sending anyone away. It
loads on a click rather than on arrival, which is worth saying plainly:
until a visitor asks for the calendar, no third party is contacted at all.

We will not oversell what that is. The calendar itself is a booking
provider's, embedded, not a reservation engine we wrote, and on a specimen
it points at a demonstration account rather than a real salon's diary. The
claim this page makes is narrower than "we built booking" and more useful:
the visitor never leaves, and the business keeps its own front door.

## Two languages, because this coast has two

The site ships in English at the root and Vietnamese under `/vi/`. Not a
translation widget bolted to the corner of the page and not a machine
guessing from a browser header. Both are real pages, built at deploy time,
each with its own URL, and a visitor changes language by clicking, never by
being sniffed and sorted.

This is a genre argument, not a technology one. Ocean Springs sits between
the Biloxi and Bayou La Batre communities, and a salon here plausibly cuts
hair for both. Vietnamese on a Gulf Coast salon site is not an
internationalization feature. It is the same instinct as putting the
prices on the page: say the thing in the language the person in your chair
actually reads.

The translation reaches every page a visitor came to read. Service
descriptions, the about page, the FAQ, the testimonials, the nav, the days
of the week, the month names under each review. The legal pages are the one
deliberate exception and stay in English. A second language that covers the
marketing copy and then drops the visitor back into English at the first
useful page is worse than not offering one, so the line gets drawn at the
pages somebody actually reads before deciding.

One caveat, stated here because it belongs on the record and not in a
footnote: the Vietnamese on this specimen is demo-grade and has not had a
native-speaker pass. That bar is fine for a demonstration that search
engines are told not to index. It is not the bar for a business whose
customers actually read the language, and on a real build that copy goes to
a human who speaks it before it goes anywhere near a visitor.

## Correctly, not generically, a salon

A search engine is told exactly what kind of business this is: a beauty
salon, specifically, not a generic storefront and not a medical spa, which
is a different category carrying a different set of rules this business
doesn't need. The listing carries the address, the geography, the real
opening hours day by day, and the services with their prices attached.

Getting the category right is what makes the listing look right once it
starts showing up. Search engines have a shape they expect a salon to
describe itself in, and a site that fills that shape properly gets to look
like a salon in the results instead of a URL with a name on it.

## Nothing loading that nobody asked for

This specimen has no marketing analytics configured at all. No pixel, no
ad tag, no conversion tracker. Not one disabled and left dormant in the
page waiting to be switched on, but genuinely absent, which is why the
homepage carries zero third-party scripts. That posture is available to
any business that simply does not want ad-tech in the building, and more
of them want it than get offered it.

What does run is a first-party visit counter, on our own domain, which
counts a page view and knows nothing else about who you are. Consent
controls on this specimen are set the way most Gulf Coast small businesses
would set them, with a visible banner and a decline that takes effect
immediately. Where a business needs the stricter posture instead, because
of its jurisdiction or because it simply prefers it, the same site can be
configured so that nothing fires until a visitor says yes, and so that a
browser's own do-not-track signal is honored without being asked twice.

The point is not that one setting is correct everywhere. It is that the
setting is a decision the business gets to make, on the record, rather than
a default inherited from whichever tag manager showed up first.

## The same floor, a different building

None of the things that make a restaurant site hard are here. No cart, no
kitchen board, no payment to authorize. The bar is identical anyway.
Every form is protected against bots. A full modern security header set
rides on every response, including strict transport security and a refusal
to be framed.

Speed and accessibility are not left to good intentions either. Every page
has to clear a performance, accessibility, and best-practices score of 95
before the build is allowed to pass, and the check walks the built output
rather than a hand-written list of pages, so a page added next month cannot
quietly skip it. A standard that is only enforced when somebody remembers
to look is not a standard.

Search ranking is the one score this particular site does not post, and
that is on purpose. It is a demonstration, not a business, so it asks
search engines to ignore it entirely, and a page that asks not to be
indexed cannot score as though it wanted to be. On a real build that
instruction is simply absent, and the number comes back with the others.

That bar is not a restaurant feature or a salon feature. It is the floor
every small business site should be built on, whether it sells dinner or a
haircut, and the reason to put a specimen like this on the internet is to
make the floor visible to somebody who has only ever been quoted a price
for a business card with a phone number on it.
