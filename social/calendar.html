Days and times: why Tuesday–Thursday stays

You asked not to post Tue/Wed/Thu unless that's actually the optimal schedule. For this audience it is. The 2026 timing studies (Sprout Social's engagement analysis, Buffer's post-level data, Hootsuite's) agree on the shape: Wednesday is the strongest single day, Thursday second, Tuesday close behind; Friday and Saturday are the weakest. Weekends earn a slot only for consumer and event content, and this calendar no longer carries any. So the three days stay, and each week's strongest visual lands on Wednesday.

Evening posting is not a compromise. Buffer's 2026 data has 6–9 PM beating mornings on Instagram most days, and Facebook shows a second daily peak in the early evening — which is when a shop owner finally sits down with the phone. Since you'll be posting after work at first, the defaults lean into that instead of fighting it.

The five-second test

P4 · Educational: how local businesses stand out

Core idea: A local business website has one job — tell a stranger what to do next: call, book, order, or walk in. Hand your phone to someone who has never been to your place, give them five seconds, and ask what they were supposed to do. Design is the strategy, not the decoration. This is the month's most shareable post; pin it on all three platforms once it's up.

XSingle post + image9:00 AM CT (scheduled)
Post copy

Hand your phone to a stranger with your website open. Give them five seconds. Can they tell what to do next — call, book, order, walk in?

If not, the site isn't working. It's decorating.

Pick one action. Put it where the thumb lands. Everything else is second.

Here's a test you can run today. It costs nothing.

Pull up your website on your phone and hand it to someone who's never been to your place — a neighbor, a cousin, the person behind you in line. Give them five seconds. Then ask: what were you supposed to do there?

If they hesitate, that's the whole problem. Not the colors. Not the logo. Not whether the photos are new. A visitor arrives with a question — can I get a table, do y'all do this, are you open right now — and the site's only job is to answer it and point to the next step. Call, book, order, walk in. One of those, big enough to hit with a thumb, before anyone scrolls.

Most local business sites we look at say everything and ask for nothing. The homepage welcomes you. It tells the story. It lists the services. And the phone number lives in small type at the bottom, under the copyright.

Design is strategy here, not decoration. The strategy is: decide what you want the visitor to do, then build the page so that's the easiest thing on it.

Run the five-second test this week. If you'd like a second pair of eyes on what you find, message the page with your link — no pitch, just an honest read.

Five quiet ways a website loses customers — quiet because nobody complains. They just leave.

1. The menu is a PDF. On a phone it's a pinch-and-zoom chore, Google can't read what's in it, and updating it means calling somebody. A menu that lives on the page updates in minutes and shows up when someone searches "crab cakes near me."

2. Your hours on the site don't match your hours on Google. People trust whichever one they saw first, and then they show up to a locked door. Pick one source of truth and make everything point to it.

3. "Book now" sends people to a third-party site. Every time your customer leaves your site to book, someone else owns that relationship — and usually charges you for it. Booking that stays on your own site is possible for most businesses, and cheaper than you'd think.

4. The phone number isn't tappable. This is the ten-minute fix. On a phone, a number should be a button.

5. The homepage welcomes everyone and asks for nothing. "Welcome to our website!" is not an action. Call, book, order, walk in — pick the one you want most and make it the biggest thing on the screen.

None of these need a redesign. They need a decision and an afternoon. If you want a second pair of eyes on your site, drop the link in the comments and we'll give you an honest read — no pitch.

Why one person?

It's the question I get most, and it deserves a real answer.

For more than a decade I built software inside big organizations, and the thing I watched most closely wasn't the code. It was the path a simple change took. A business owner asks for something small — move this button, add this field — and the request passes through a salesperson, a project manager, a designer, a developer, a tester, and back. Everyone careful. Everyone a little further from the person who asked. By the time it lands, it's been translated five times.

Solo by choice doesn't mean small ambitions. It means I'll only take on what one pair of hands can do properly — and then do it properly. The best work I've ever been part of was done that way. This is the version of that work I always wanted to do for the businesses I actually visit.

Most of this work starts the same way, by the way: someone is tired of doing a thing by hand, and someone else says, "I could build that." If that first someone is you, the door's open.
